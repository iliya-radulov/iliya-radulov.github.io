In the Matter of Artificial Intelligence & the Limits of Oversight

Five witnesses called. Five questions, one each. None of them human.

During Data Ethics Week, our course put five questions on the table: GDPR, algorithmic bias in healthcare, the EU AI Act's risk tiers, and who should actually be accountable when AI goes wrong. As a bonus exercise, those questions were reassigned — one per witness — to five local RAG systems, each grounded only in one author's own indexed bibliography.

They aren't asked to imitate an author's style. They're asked to answer using nothing but that author's actual words, retrieved and reasoned over locally. What follows is what came back.

Kurt Vonnegut

42 volumes indexed English Slaughterhouse-Five & the complete works
Question put to the witness So it goes with AI regulation — should we trust the machines, or the people who build them?
Testimony
Transcript Review.

So it goes. The question echoes the eternal struggle between free will and fatalism, a dichotomy that Vonnegut so astutely explored in his works. In the grand tapestry of human existence, can we truly trust the very creations we've brought into being? The machines, with their cold calculations and precision, or the people who built them, with their flawed morality and biases? Consider the builder of the roads, from While Mortals Sleep, who fritters away his free time with miniature trains, only to have his fantasy land crushed by the women in his life. Or the newspaperman, also from While Mortals Sleep, forced onto a committee to judge Christmas displays, where he discovers a suspiciously ostentatious ex-con. Do these characters reveal a tendency to create and then abandon their creations, just as we do with our machines? In the context of Hocus Pocus, the devilish twins with enormous intellect hidden behind a facade of idiocy serve as a warning about the dangers of unchecked power and the hubris of human creation. Their very existence raises questions about the responsibility that comes with playing God. So, should we trust the machines or the people who build them? The answer, much like the nature of reality itself, is a complex tapestry of contradictions. We must consider the inherent flaws in both our creations and ourselves, and strive for a balance between the precision of machines and the compassion of humanity. As Vonnegut so eloquently put it, "So it goes."

Question put to the witness What would Sam Vimes say about the EU AI Act and its risk-based approach?
Testimony
Transcript Review.

Sam Vimes, being the pragmatic and cynical commander that he is, would likely say something like: "Ah, the EU AI Act. A risk-based approach, you say? Well, that's just a fancy way of saying 'we've got no idea what we're doing, so let's just throw some rules at it and hope for the best.' Mark my words, it'll be a bureaucratic nightmare, with more lawyers making money than actual safety being ensured. And what's this I hear about 'high-risk' AI systems? High-risk? What's that supposed to mean? That they're more likely to cause chaos and destruction? Well, isn't that just the definition of any government initiative? And don't even get me started on the 'transparency' requirements. Transparency, indeed. I've seen more transparency in the Ankh-Morpork city council's dealings with the Patrician. But, I suppose, in the grand tradition of 'Acquiris Quodcumque Rapis' (You Get What You Grab), we'll just have to grab at this risk-based approach and hope it doesn't end up being a mess. After all, what's a bit of chaos and destruction among friends? Now, if you'll excuse me, I have more important things to attend to. Like catching some real crooks." (From "Jingo", Discworld 21) Of course, this is just a speculative answer, but it's in line with Sam Vimes' character and his views on bureaucracy and the complexities of modern life.

Виктор Пелевин

88 volumes indexed Russian (original) Complete works, incl. the Transhumanism series
Question put to the witness Is the AI Act just another simulation of control, or a genuine step toward transhumanist ethics?
Testimony
Transcript Review.

The question of whether the AI Act is a genuine step toward transhumanist ethics or just another simulation of control is a complex one, reminiscent of the themes explored in Pelevin's "Transhumanism Inc." (Трансгуманизм Inc.). In this work, Pelevin critiques the notion of control and the ways in which those in power manipulate and shape reality. He explores the intersection of technology, politics, and human consciousness, revealing the ways in which these forces can be used to create simulations of control, masking the true nature of power. In this context, the AI Act can be seen as a tool for shaping the narrative around AI and its potential applications. On the surface, it appears to be a genuine effort to establish guidelines and regulations for AI development, but upon closer inspection, it may be revealed to be a simulation of control, designed to maintain the status quo and further the interests of those in power. Pelevin's "Transhumanism Inc." suggests that the line between simulation and reality is often blurred, and that those who wield power are skilled in creating elaborate illusions that mask their true intentions. In this sense, the AI Act may be viewed as just another simulation of control, designed to legitimize and perpetuate the existing power structures. However, it is also possible that the AI Act represents a genuine attempt to establish a new framework for AI development, one that prioritizes human values and ethics. Pelevin's work often explores the tension between the potential of technology to liberate humanity and the risk of it being used to further entrap us in a web of control. Ultimately, the true nature of the AI Act remains unclear, and it is up to each individual to critically evaluate the intentions behind this initiative and to consider the potential implications for human consciousness and society as a whole. As the character of Viktor Bartoškin remarks in "Transhumanism Inc.," "The main thing is not to get lost in the details, but to see the overall picture." It is essential to consider the larger context and the motivations behind the AI Act, rather than simply accepting it at face value. In this sense, the AI Act can be seen as a reflection of the transhumanist ideals that Pelevin explores in his work, highlighting the tension between the potential for human liberation and the risk of further entrapment in a web of control.

No verdict is reached here. Five witnesses, five answers, one open question about who — or what — gets to decide when a system is trustworthy enough to leave unsupervised. Read against each other, the disagreements are the actual finding.
