The journey

I recently got my first 3D printer and have been learning the ropes by printing parts for other projects — Pi Cam boxes, cases for Pi 4 / 5 / 3, flex cam mounts, tilt‑pan mounts, and components for my SO‑ARM101 robot arm. After dozens of downloaded models, I felt ready to try my own design.

Design & CAD

My first self‑designed part was a simple stencil — just a silhouette extruded to 3D. Easy enough. But the real challenge came next: designing replacement belt holders for an old Makeblock laser cutter (circa 2015) whose original PA (polyamide) parts had become brittle as glass and were falling apart.

I spent a few hours learning FreeCAD. As an open‑source fan I can honestly say — FreeCAD is fantastic for this kind of mechanical part design. Fully parametric, exports STL cleanly, and runs well on both Mac and Linux.

01Measure the broken part

With a Mitutoyo caliper I measured the crumbling originals. The plate is symmetric — all four wheel holes sit exactly 4.1 mm from each edge, and all holes are 4.1 mm diameter (M4 clearance). Overall plate: 50.6 × 43.4 × 6 mm.

02Sketch in FreeCAD

Started with a fully‑constrained rectangle, padded to 6 mm, then added the four corner holes as a second sketch on the top face — pocketed through‑all. The hex nut traps (4 mm deep from the back face) came last.

03Hex nut traps — the interesting bit

The original part used hexagonal pockets to capture M4 nuts so they can't spin during assembly — a classic 3D‑print trick. I modelled them as 8 mm across‑flats hex pockets, 4 mm deep. Lesson learned: next version uses 8.2 mm — PETG shrinks slightly and the nuts were a tight push.

Print & result

Sliced in Creality Print, sent wirelessly to the Ender 3 V3 Plus. The printer ran its automatic bed‑levelling routine, and ~31 minutes later the parts were done. Both pieces came out clean — holes in the right positions, hex pockets functional, overall dimensions spot on.

With a little persuasion the M4 nuts went in. It's surprisingly satisfying to see something you designed on screen become a physical object that actually works.

✅ First original design → successfully printed & test‑fitted against the Makeblock frame. PETG will outlast the original PA — the material that caused the failure in the first place.

Why PETG over the original PA?

The original parts were moulded in PA (polyamide / nylon). PA becomes brittle with age and UV exposure — exactly what happened here after ~10 years. PETG is tougher, more impact‑resistant, and holds up better long‑term for indoor mechanical parts. The replacement should outlast the machine itself.

The next challenge

Now that the mechanical parts are ready, the real work begins: getting the laser cutter back to life. The original control software (mLaser) hasn't been updated since 2017 and is Windows‑only. The plan is to run it from a Raspberry Pi 4 on Linux, or find a modern open‑source alternative.

Reviving the Makeblock laser — mLaser last updated 2017. Hardware is solid; the control stack needs some love. Candidate: run the open‑source repo on the Pi 4, or replace with LaserWeb / LightBurn if the firmware speaks GRBL.

The original repo is still on GitHub — Makeblock-official/MLaser (2017, open source). If you've revived old Makeblock gear, I'd love to hear your approach.
