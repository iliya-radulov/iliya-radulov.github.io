Forecasting Daily NO2 Concentrations in London

A comparative study of baseline, ARIMA and Prophet models

Iliya Radulov · November 2026

Summary

Fourteen years of daily nitrogen dioxide measurements from one London monitoring station were used to test how far simple and classical models can forecast pollution levels. The yearly heating and weather cycle turned out to be the only signal that reliably improves a forecast. Prophet, which models that cycle directly, won every evaluation. Holidays, the COVID lockdown and the ULEZ expansion added almost nothing.

1Introduction

Nitrogen dioxide (NO2) is a persistent public health concern in dense cities. Most of it in London comes from road traffic and gas heating. Over the last decade the city has pushed hard to reduce it, through bus and taxi fleet modernisation and the Ultra Low Emission Zone (ULEZ). Long-term measurements show the result: concentrations have roughly halved since 2010.

This study asks whether that declining, seasonal signal can be forecast at a daily resolution. It starts with simple baselines that set a performance floor, then adds complexity step by step with ARIMA, SARIMA and Prophet. The goal is to find the most accurate model and also to understand what is actually predictable in the series: do the trend and seasonality outweigh the day-to-day noise?

2Data and setup

The data are daily mean NO2 concentrations from the London Air Quality Network station KC1 in North Kensington, an urban background site. The series runs from 1 January 2010 to 31 December 2023.

TargetDaily mean NO2, µg/m³
Training2010 – 2022 (13 years)
Hold-out testCalendar year 2023

Evaluation metric. Models are compared by mean absolute error (MAE) in µg/m³, with RMSE as a secondary check. MAPE is not used. On clean-air days NO2 can fall close to zero, so the percentage error explodes and one calm week can dominate the score. MAE stays in physical units and is easy to read: an MAE of 7 means the forecast is off by 7 µg/m³ on an average day.

Evaluation schemes. Three schemes are used. The baseline, ARIMA and Prophet sections score a single forecast of all of 2023. Walk-forward cross-validation checks whether a result holds across many forecast origins. The final comparison runs all models from 12 origins spread through 2023, each forecasting 30 days ahead.

Limitation. All results come from one station. Roadside sites, or areas with different local sources, may behave differently, so the conclusions should be replicated across more of the network before they are generalised to London as a whole.

3Baselines

Five simple forecasts set the floor that any real model has to beat:

  • Mean predicts the average of the whole training history for every future day.
  • Naïve repeats the last observed value.
  • Drift extends the line between the first and last training values.
  • Seasonal naïve (m = 7) repeats the value from the same weekday one week earlier.
  • Seasonal naïve (m = 365) repeats the value from the same date one year earlier.

The comparison between the two seasonal versions gave the first surprise. For urban pollution the usual assumption is that the weekly traffic cycle, busy weekdays against quieter weekends, is the dominant pattern. At KC1 the yearly version clearly wins. The strongest seasonal signal at this station is the annual cycle driven by winter heating and winter weather, not the weekly commute. The Mean model is the worst because it predicts a flat line at the long-run average, which sits well above recent levels after a decade of decline.

4ARIMA and SARIMA

ARIMA models forecast from the autocorrelation in the series: today's value depends on recent values and recent errors. A small grid of orders was searched on AIC, separately with and without first differencing. The best differenced model, ARIMA(4,1,3), reached an MAE of 8.13 on 2023, beating every baseline by more than 2 µg/m³.

The gain is concentrated at the start of the forecast. For roughly the first month, ARIMA follows the local level of the series better than any baseline. After that it settles towards a nearly constant value and behaves like a random walk. This is expected: ARIMA has no memory of the yearly cycle, so once the short-term autocorrelation has decayed, it has nothing left to say about next winter.

A seasonal ARIMA with a weekly period, SARIMA(1,1,1)(1,1,0)7, removed about half of the remaining autocorrelation at weekly lags. It could not address the yearly cycle that the baselines had shown to matter most. A 365-day seasonal period is impractical in SARIMA. It needs very long seasonal lags, is slow to fit and tends to overfit.

The prediction intervals also become physically meaningless after a few days. They widen to cover negative concentrations, which cannot occur, and span a range of roughly −20 to +80 µg/m³. Intervals that wide give no practical help for planning around high-pollution days.

Does the result hold across many origins?

A single test year can flatter or punish a model by chance, so the comparison was repeated with walk-forward cross-validation: train on a rolling 365-day window, forecast the next 30 days, move forward and repeat. A rolling window treats every fold equally, which suits a series whose level changes over time better than an expanding window that keeps old, higher values.

Under this scheme the ranking changed. The Mean baseline came first with an MAE of 7.62, ahead of ARIMA at 9.43 and Naïve at 9.60. With only one year of training data, the mean stays close to the current level of the series, so the long-term decline no longer hurts it. ARIMA(2,1,0) without drift behaved almost exactly like a random walk over 30 days. The 0.17 difference from Naïve is within noise. For horizons of a month or more, a well-chosen average is as good as an autoregressive model on this series.

5Prophet

Prophet, developed at Meta, treats a time series as the sum of a trend, seasonal terms and holiday effects. Its seasonalities are Fourier series, so a 365-day cycle costs a handful of parameters instead of the long seasonal lags SARIMA would need. That is exactly the component the baselines identified as most important.

The model was built up in four steps, adding one feature at a time:

  • Baseline: piecewise linear trend with yearly and weekly seasonality, multiplicative mode.
  • + Bank holidays: England's public holidays.
  • + COVID lockdown: a custom holiday block covering the spring 2020 lockdown.
  • + ULEZ: a step regressor switched on at the October 2021 inner-London expansion.

Additive and multiplicative seasonality gave similar results. Between the early and late years the absolute winter–summer gap shrank from about 17–21 to about 11–13 µg/m³, but more slowly than the overall level fell. Neither mode describes that perfectly, and the multiplicative version was kept by a small margin.

The three added features improved MAE by less than 0.05 µg/m³ in total. Prophet's advantage comes almost entirely from its yearly seasonality.

At a background site, holidays shift daily NO2 by far less than the weather does. The lockdown fell inside the training period and did not affect 2023. The ULEZ effect is already absorbed by the trend, which has been declining for years. A model with only trend and seasonality is effectively as good as the full version.

The 80% prediction interval actually contained about 89% of observations, so Prophet's uncertainty is slightly conservative. That is the safer direction for a pollution forecast.

Cross-validation and tuning

Prophet's own rolling cross-validation (three years of initial training, then a new cutoff every 90 days, 41 cutoffs in total) gave a mean MAE of 9.17. Naïve scored 13.7 and seasonal naïve 12.6 on the same cutoffs. From 3 to 12 days ahead the error stayed between 8.4 and 9.9 with no upward drift, and the 80% interval covered 82–90% of observations. Unlike ARIMA, Prophet does not degrade with distance, because its seasonal terms are fixed patterns and do not depend on recent observations.

A grid search over the trend flexibility parameter, changepoint_prior_scale ∈ {0.005, 0.05, 0.5}, confirmed that the default of 0.05 was already optimal. Tuning changed nothing.

6Final comparison

In the final test every model forecast 30 days ahead from 12 origins spaced 30 days apart through 2023, retraining on all data available up to each origin. This gives a mean error and also a spread, which shows how consistent each model is across the year.

Prophet wins with a mean MAE of 6.86, ahead of the 365-day recent mean at 8.21. ARIMA, seasonal naïve and naïve are close together in third to fifth place. ARIMA's 98.6% coverage for an 80% interval confirms its intervals are far too wide to be useful.

The per-origin results show where the advantage comes from. In January, February and December the persistence models (Naïve and ARIMA) miss badly, with errors up to 21 µg/m³, because a winter level shift or a stagnant high-pressure spell is not in their recent history. Prophet's yearly term expects those months to be high and keeps its error at 8–14. From spring to early autumn, concentrations are lower and the atmosphere mixes better. All models then converge to errors of about 3–7, and Prophet's lead mostly disappears.

7Conclusion

The yearly cycle of heating and weather is the only reliably predictable component of daily mean NO2 at KC1. Prophet was the best model in every evaluation because it captures that cycle cheaply and flexibly. Its success did not come from policy or calendar effects: bank holidays, the COVID lockdown and the ULEZ regressor together changed the error by less than 0.05 µg/m³.

Daily pollution is still hard to forecast. Past the first few days, the error is dominated by weather, which none of these models can see. Even the best model is off by about 7 µg/m³ on an average day, and much more during winter episodes. A useful operational forecast would need numerical weather prediction inputs (wind speed, boundary-layer height, temperature) as external regressors, not just the history of the pollutant itself. That is the natural next step for this work, together with repeating the analysis across more London stations.
