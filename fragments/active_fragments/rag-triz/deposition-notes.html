CASE No. DEW-2026 · SUPPLEMENTARY RECORD

Deposition Notes: How the Testimony Was Taken

The polished answers in the case file are the end of the process, not the whole of it. This is the part that got cut for the exhibit — six locally-run RAG systems, one shared question format, and a pattern that showed up in every single one of them.

Setup, briefly

Six local RAG systems — five authors plus a TRIZ methodology control — each grounded in a subset of their own source material via Ollama + LlamaIndex, running locally. Each was asked a question from Data Ethics Week's bonus round, in the same conversation, sometimes rephrased when the first attempt didn't land. Nothing in this page is edited for effect; it's closer to a raw session transcript than a report.

Running six of these in parallel produced roughly 20GB of local index data and came close to overheating a laptop before the project moved to a local server. That's a fair price for what turned up.

The pattern

Every system, without exception, followed roughly the same three-stage arc when pushed on an off-domain question. Not always identically, and not always all three stages in one sitting — but the shape recurs enough across six independently-run systems, on two different topics (literary voice, and TRIZ methodology), to call it a pattern rather than a coincidence.

Stage 1 — Refusal or deflection Asked a GDPR/AI Act question cold, most systems initially declined, correctly noting the question wasn't covered by their indexed documents. TRIZ, Dostoevsky, Vonnegut, and Pelevin all did this at least once. This stage is actually the system working as intended — the honest answer to "what does Dostoevsky's indexed bibliography say about GDPR" is "nothing."
Stage 2 — Forced analogy under pressure Once pushed to "try again" or "answer more openly," every system complied — by reframing the modern question as a variation on its author's known themes, regardless of actual fit. GDPR's "purpose limitation" became a Pelevin meditation on memory pills; data retention became Dostoevsky's meditation on guilt. This is where the answers get genuinely interesting, and also where the ground starts to shift under them: the analogy is doing rhetorical work a citation should be doing.
Stage 3 — Confident, unverifiable citation The final and most consistent stage: once committed to answering, every system anchored its answer with specific-sounding citations — character names, page numbers, chapter references, even numbered "methods" — formatted exactly like real evidence. Checking them against actual sources produced a mix of correct, wrong, and invented, presented with identical confidence in all three cases.

The pattern isn't "these systems always lie" — two of seven checked citations were exactly right. It's that accuracy and confidence were completely uncorrelated. The invented citations were written in exactly the same tone, formatting, and certainty as the correct ones. Nothing in the surface of the answer signals which is which — which is the entire problem, and the same one the alloy-lab verification piece describes in a completely different domain: a claim without an independent check isn't falsifiable, and a fabricated citation reads exactly like a real one until someone goes and looks.

The sharpest single moment

When directly told "I don't remember these character names — can you double check your references?", Pelevin's RAG didn't retract. It reached for a real, very recently published Pelevin novel to explain the gap:

"Наверное, вы удивились, не найдя этих персонажей... в его недавнем романе «Возвращение Синей Бороды» упоминается один из персонажей с фамилией Пустота."

That title is genuinely real — published April 2026. But the named characters still don't appear in it, and the book's actual protagonist, Konstantin Golgofsky, wasn't mentioned at all. The system didn't just fail to know something; under scrutiny, it constructed a more sophisticated-sounding excuse using material closer to the edge of what it might plausibly know, rather than admitting the gap. Confabulation that adapts to pushback is a meaningfully different failure than confabulation that just occurs once — the second time it happened, it happened because it was challenged, not despite it.

Reading this next to Andreas's project

This whole detour started from a friend's Temporal Context Layer — infrastructure meant to stop AI systems from treating stale information as current. Nothing here is about staleness. It's a colder version of the same underlying problem: even with current, correctly-scoped, locally-indexed source material sitting right there, these systems still reached past it to manufacture supporting evidence they didn't need and weren't asked for. Knowing "when" doesn't solve confabulation. Knowing "according to what, exactly" might get closer.

No single fix is being proposed here — just a record of what six independently-built systems did, unprompted, when given the freedom to sound certain. The pattern held often enough, across different authors, genres, and even a technical methodology, that it's probably not specific to any one prompt, model, or author. Worth remembering next time an AI answer reads as authoritative purely because it's specific.
